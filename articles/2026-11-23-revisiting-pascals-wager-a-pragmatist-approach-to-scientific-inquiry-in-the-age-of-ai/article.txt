[1] (P) If OpenAI CEO Sam Altman is to be believed, “we are past the event horizon, the take-off [toward singularity] has started.” In other words, according to Altman (and many AI luminaries and advocates), artificial general intelligence (AGI) is inevitable. Hundreds of billions of dollars funneled to OpenAI and adjacent ventures, and Altman’s engagement with world leaders lend his words considerable weight. Few powerful firms appear willing to bet against this narrative of inevitability—despite evidence that progress may have stalled or at least slowed.
[2] (P) The reasoning undergirding the reluctance to challenge the AGI narrative (often echoed by prominent figures including Altman himself), resembles a modern-day Pascal’s Wager, at least structurally in a logical sense: If AI will transform everything, those who adopt it early are likely to thrive; those who do not risk irrelevance. What it there to lose in betting on its success? In both the theological and technological versions, the wager is framed as a rational choice under uncertainty: Stake something finite now in order to avoid catastrophic loss and to secure potentially enormous gains.
[3] (P) Yet, this wager is neither an innocent rational calculus and nor is it value neutral. It is driven by considerations of private gain as well as fear of missing out while others capitalize on the opportunity. The underlying assumption here is that private gain is desirable, even at the expense of social good. This argument is seductive, especially in academia, where the pressure to publish is relentless and those who fail to do so presumably fall by the wayside or do not get tenure. Large language models (LLMs) can summarize papers, generate hypotheses, and even draft manuscripts. Consequently, researchers who embrace the use of LLMs gain substantial advantages over those who resist it, at least in efficiency. The use of AI in scholarship seems to be becoming increasingly normalized and even encouraged for a wide variety of tasks ranging from editing to “thinking.” At the same time, there is mounting evidence that LLMs do not “reason” in any meaningful sense and hallucinations remain an intractable problem. Other researchers worry about what it means to be a scientist in the age of AI if thinking is increasingly outsourced to bots, and whether overreliance on such tools erodes skills such as critical reading, argumentation, and judgment.
[4] (P) The result is not a clean split into two hostile camps, but emerging fault lines within the scientific community. On one end of the spectrum are “believers” who are eager to integrate AI into nearly every facet of their work. They point to impressive applications such as designing new proteins, generating novel research ideas, or decoding historical texts as evidence that resistance is irrational. On the other end are researchers who remain skeptical of the hype. They highlight indications that some kinds of AI development are hitting a wall and emphasize the pernicious implications of premature adoption, including loss of critical thinking and overconfidence in opaque systems. The same applies to the shifting boundary between public and private AI research. As more development moves into corporate labs bound by trade secrets and competitive pressures, norms of open science are strained. The developments are concerning—science thrives on engagement between scientists, but only when it is grounded in mutual respect and a shared commitment to evidence with the aim that a provisional consensus is reached. The loss or dilution of scientific communities poses a challenge for science.
[5] (H) Pascal’s Wager in the Age of AI
[6] (P) To make sense of these developments, it is helpful to recall the structure—not the theology—of Pascal’s original wager. In its simplest form, the wager presents four possibilities: God exists or does not exist; one believes or does not believe. Belief combined with God’s existence supposedly yields infinite gain (salvation), whereas disbelief combined with God’s existence yields infinite loss (damnation); if God does not exist, the gains or losses are finite. From a purely calculative perspective, staking one’s finite life on belief seems rational.
[7] (P) Altman’s rhetoric and the wider AGI discourse can be read as a secularized version of this matrix. One can adopt AI tools and narratives of AGI’s inevitability, or one can resist them. If AGI truly transforms everything, early adopters are depicted as enjoying extraordinary rewards—funding, influence, and prestige—while laggards’ risk institutional irrelevance. If AGI does not materialize as promised, the story goes, early adopters have sacrificed only finite resources: some time, money, and perhaps a few misdirected research efforts. Using this framing, it appears rational to “believe” in AGI’s inevitability and act accordingly. But this discourse that structurally mirrors Pascal’s wager obscures at least three features that matter for science.
[8] (P) First, Pascal’s original argument concerned belief in a metaphysical claim whose truth value could not be empirically settled. In contrast, the utility of AI in research—whether LLMs improve peer review, or whether particular models aid protein design—is, in principle, subject to evidence and evaluation. What looks like “belief” in AGI is often better understood as a set of expectations about technological trajectories, institutions, and markets. Here, pragmatism is helpful: for Charles Sanders Peirce, belief is not a purely mental state but is associated with a “habit of action.” Whether we label it belief or expectation, the practical issue is which habits scientists should adopt given conditions of uncertainty
[9] (P) Second, the costs of “believing” in AI are not limited to individual researchers’ time or funding. If AI tools are adopted uncritically, they may reshape entire epistemic practices ranging from how data are collected and how claims are justified to how credit is assigned, in ways that are difficult to reverse. Unlike Pascal’s imagined sinner who can simply repent, scientific institutions cannot easily roll back infrastructures, incentives, and norms once they have been reconfigured around particular technologies.
[10] (P) Third, the wager’s payoff structure is not neutral. In contemporary AI discourse, the implied “infinite gain” is overwhelmingly private: monopoly profits, strategic dominance, or personal success. Losses, by contrast, are often socialized—borne by those excluded from decision making, or by people that must live with the consequences of biased systems, environmental costs, or weakened scientific institutions.
[11] (H) The Way Forward
[12] (P) Pragmatism suggests a different question from “Should we believe in AGI?” or “Is it rational to bet on AI?” Instead, it asks: In which technological future do we want to believe, given the values we hold and the forms of life we wish to sustain? Beliefs about the future of AI are not value neutral; they are intertwined with commitments about what counts as progress, whose interests should be served, and which risks are acceptable.
[13] (P) Disinterestedness is particularly relevant in an environment suffused with AI wagers. A scientist who is personally invested in an AI company, for instance, may find it difficult to evaluate contrary evidence, just as a theologian might struggle to assess arguments undermining their faith. By contrast, a disinterested scientist aims to keep inquiry open, allowing evidence and shared standards to shape beliefs rather than precommitting to AI’s success or failure.
[14] (P) Researchers who embrace disinterestedness can evaluate the use of AI in science—whether for text editing, coding, data analysis, or discovery—not by asking, “What do I stand to gain if this bet pays off?” but “How does this use affect the integrity of the institution of science, which is meant to serve the greater good?” Some uses of AI, such as grammar and style editing, may raise relatively modest epistemic concerns while offering accessibility benefits; others, such as automated hypothesis generation or analysis of sensitive datasets, raise deeper questions about reproducibility, bias, and the distribution of epistemic authority.
[15] (P) Because scientific fields differ in methods, stakes, and histories, plural, context-sensitive standards are required. Blanket policies that mandate AI use or ban it outright risk both overreach and oversight. In some domains, communities may reach a broad consensus that certain AI tools are acceptable adjuncts to established practices; in others, they may conclude that heavy reliance on such tools would erode core disciplinary skills or introduce unacceptable vulnerabilities. What matters, from a pragmatist and Mertonian perspective, is that these decisions emerge from open, inclusive deliberation rather than from one-sided wagers by actors with concentrated power. To recognize and accommodate variations in beliefs, we need to create (more) spaces for thoughtful disagreement where all voices raised in good faith are welcome.
[16] (P) This is where epistemic humility becomes crucial. No one—Altman included—has all the answers about where AI is headed or what its long-term effects will be. Uncertainty should not paralyze inquiry, but it should temper grandiose claims and encourage careful experimentation. Precisely because the stakes are high, scientists and institutions must proceed critically and collaboratively, be precise in their language, and resist overstating what current AI systems can do.
[17] (P) Finally, the underlying incentive structures in academia require reconsideration. AI adoption offers an opportunity for an “audit” of sorts. In grant writing, for instance, including an AI component can function as a Pascalian bet: the additional effort required is modest, yet the perceived chance of securing scarce funds is substantially higher than for proposals that refuse the AI frame—though evaluators may react the opposite way, welcoming a proposal precisely because it does not follow the herd. If researchers feel compelled to add AI components to projects simply to stay competitive, this suggests that incentives for publications and visibility may be crowding out the vocation of science as a public good. Rebalancing incentives away from sheer volume of output and toward robustness, openness, and social relevance could reduce fraud, encourage deeper engagement with societal problems, and make it easier for researchers to resist wager-based hype.
[18] (P) The consequences of how scientists engage with AI extend beyond the university walls. To the extent that scientists abdicate their duty of disinterestedness, trust in the institution of science will be undercut, and our voices might be treated with cynicism often reserved for business moguls. The ripple effect of premature adoption of AI universities—driven by a fear of missing out—is already being felt with even students raising an alarm. After all, what good is a degree that essentially reflects proficiency in using ChatGPT?
[19] (P) To be sure, this dynamic concerning the implications of embracing new technology is not new. Throughout history, technological revolutions have been accompanied by fervent belief and equally fervent skepticism. What makes the current moment unique is the speed and opacity of AI development as well as the concentration of wealth associated with its development. Very often, ardent advocates of AI adoption (in the Silicon Valley) have their fortunes tied with it, which, at the very least, suggests that there might be a conflict of interest. In addition, the motives of at least some of these powerful advocates are not as pure as they seem. To assume otherwise is foolish as James Madison warned us in Federalist Paper no. 51.
[20] (H) Conclusion
[21] (P) According to Sam Altman, “Successful people build companies. More successful people build countries. The most successful people build religions […] It appears to me that the best way to build a religion is actually to build a company.” The rush to embrace AI reflects a familiar Silicon Valley ethos of “moving fast and breaking things,” underwritten by the assumption that whatever is broken can later be fixed. Yet, as the nursery rhyme Humpty Dumpty reminds us, some things, once broken, cannot be put back together. Rolling back a line of code or withdrawing a model does not erase the harms it may have caused—to individuals wrongly classified, to public trust in institutions, or to the norms of scientific practice.
[22] (P) Altman’s take may, at first blush, seem to offer a compelling narrative for embracing AI: in a world of uncertainty, why not bet on the side that promises transformation? But a pragmatist perspective exposes the wager’s hidden premises about who benefits, who bears the risks, and which futures become thinkable. The future of AI is open ended and mutable; it is not simply given by any one person’s imagination, however influential.
[23] (P) If science is to serve the public good in an age of rapid AI advancement, scholars and institutions must move beyond wager-based hype or fearful retreat. They must instead engage in open and critical deliberation about which technological futures to pursue, how to align them with widely shared values, and when to say “no” even in the face of seductive promises. That requires reaffirming disinterestedness, epistemic humility, and a commitment not to block the way of inquiry. Only then can scientific institutions resist becoming mere chapels in someone else’s Church of AI.

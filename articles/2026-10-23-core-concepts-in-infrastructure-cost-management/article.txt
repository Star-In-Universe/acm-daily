[3] (P) When discussing and comparing on-premise and cloud deployment patterns a decade ago, there would have been a straight line drawn from the first to the second: On-premise was the past and cloud computing was the future, even if people weren’t entirely sure what “cloud computing” was at the time. It is worth re-examining the strengths and weaknesses of both deployment modalities, especially given some notable movements back to on-premise deployments.
[4] (H) On-Premise Cost Management
[5] (P) At the risk of stating the obvious, “on-premise” in the context of this post refers to deployments either within an organization’s physical premises (office or datacenter) or a third party co-located commercial datacenter. Equipment might be purchased or leased, but being on-premise generally refers not just to bare metal servers, but to racks, cables, power supplies, top of rack networking, routers, cooling, raised floors, physical security, and everything else that goes with running a functioning and stable computing environment.
[6] (P) On-premise deployments are sometimes viewed as inflexible and static; however, they can be quite adaptable when commodity hardware is paired with infrastructure as service frameworks and container frameworks for automated deployments of configurations and software releases. Being comparatively static is true enough as new equipment needs time to be provisioned, but the “static-ness” of the environment is point-in-time, not a permanent condition. The top environment management and scaling challenges are typically:
[7] (L) Choice / Lifespan of Hardware Especially with purchased equipment, decisions regarding hardware (CPU/GPU, storage, networking) have years of impact, both in terms of technical solution delivery and financials (capital expenses, depreciation).
[8] (L) Dev and QA Environments Sizing a production environment is one matter, but properly sizing lower environments can be a challenge—specifically justifying the budgets and proposed/expected utilization to management.
[9] (L) Shared Environment Management Data and analytic environments are designed to bring data together across sources and teams, but ensuring everybody gets their fair share of resources (and is appropriately paying for it) isn’t always easy.
[10] (L) Site Failover and Disaster RecoveryThis is a particularly hard problem because a fully built-out environment just sitting around waiting to get used is something that most organizations cannot afford. This all too often turns into a paper exercise for most organizations.
[11] (P) There is a benefit to the clarity that comes with knowing what was racked and the associated costs. What you see in the racks is exactly what you have.
[12] (H) Cloud Cost Management
[13] (P) Given the complexities of managing on-premise environments, it can be tempting to assume that moving to the cloud will be an easier path because someone else can manage the infrastructure. As with so many things in life the answer is “it depends,” and it especially depends on which pain points are prioritized and which specific cloud features are being leveraged. There is truth in the saying “the cloud is just somebody else’s computer,” but this does not adequately capture the complexity of public cloud platforms like Azure, AWS, and Google Cloud, which are not merely a sets of servers in somebody else’s datacenter, but a vast array of services, many of which are provided by the cloud platform vendor, and some are provided by third parties.

[16] (H) Service Utilization
[17] (P) Looking closer at the example screenshot provides a useful summary of services, providing a stack-rank of cost categories: Virtual Machines in first place, and Storage in second place. This is useful insight, but this also raises further questions:
[18] (L) Virtual Machines. Money is clearly being spent on compute, but on how many VMs? What are they doing? Are they fully utilized?
[19] (L) Storage. “Storage” in cloud computing can mean everything from local storage to VMs to cloud storage services, and charges can be accrued for writing, storing, and reading data. What exactly is being stored where, and at what cost? Storage services also have optional replication and redundancy capabilities available for up-charges.
[20] (P) None of these initial questions address how well this cloud-hosted solution is functioning. Performance diagnostics and tuning are enormous topics outside the scope of this post, but should still be mentioned because they wind up invariably having a cost impact somewhere. So many questions from a single pie chart! Getting access to diagnostic logs, as well as detailed billing records, are highly recommended for deeper analysis.
[21] (P) It should also be clear by this point that pretty much all the core on-premise challenges still remain in the cloud, but there are just a more tools that can be brought to bear. One construct is to configure appropriate “resource groups” around services and provisioned resources, which can align on major groups of functionality for solutions. Such reporting can be seen in the lower right-hand corner of the high-level cost dashboard, though in the example data nearly all the costs are rolling up to a single resource group. Resource and activity tagging can also be used for more detailed breakdowns (e.g., function = ABC, department = XYZ).
[22] (P) First-generation cloud deployments generally looked like the above and consisted primarily of VMs and and storage. Today, however, higher-level services are often driving the largest expenditures—especially data and analytic services (for example, Databricks and Snowflake)—and this presents another level of management and observability requirements. A fundamental challenge when building with cloud services is that it is often difficult to know exactly how much something will cost. While cloud services offer flexibility, they also introduce a level of complexity that is under-appreciated.
[23] (P) Understanding the difference between actual costs vs. amortized costs also matters. Cloud resources and services can often be purchased (or reserved) in blocks with discounts. This can be useful where workloads requirements are better understood, but it also increases complexity of reporting with respect to “cash out the door” vs. reserved resource/service utilization.
[24] (H) Service-Detail Utilization
[25] (P) To understand what is happening within any given cloud service will require access to service-specific activity and billing records, as well as a thorough understanding of the constructs of the service. Using Databricks as an example, there are major service components such as:
[26] (L) Unity Catalog — the overarching catalog for assets (data and code).
[27] (L) Compute — classic compute for notebooks, which execute in the customer account and accrue cloud VM and Storage charges plus Databricks runtime charges, and “serverless,” which executes entirely in the Databricks control plane but at a higher rate of Databricks runtime charges.
[28] (L) SQL Warehousing — classic (customer account), serverless.
[29] (L) AI functions — SQL functions, Genie spaces for LLMs, coding assistants, etc.
[30] (P) As all of this may execute entirely within a single cloud resource group, proper tagging becomes critical to differentiate activity and billing from a variety of teams, workloads, and stages (for example, Dev/QA/Prod). Otherwise, developers and administrators will be flying blind. The cost summary of the service will be visible, of course, but that isn’t enough to manage a solution, much less an enterprise rollout.
[31] (P) Adding to the pressure is that it often is not easy to see what cloud-based solutions cost until they have been built. Service-level utilization analysis and tagging is truly a skill within a skill.
[32] (H) Haven’t We Been Here Before?
[33] (P) Today people can joke about a server running under someone’s desk, but up until roughly the 1980s that would have been highly aspirational, if not science fiction. In the very old days, computing time often was leased on shared systems because only the largest organizations could afford not just to own their own computers but also to build out and operate datacenters to house them. Owning and operating one’s own computing environment represented not only a significant strategic advantage for purpose-built systems, but also operational control and stability. There were good reasons on-premise systems deployments were preferred by so many, once they became economically feasible.
[34] (P) Public cloud computing appeared in the late 2000s and took hold the following decade. When dynamic resource provisioning and scaling is combined with the wide breadth of services available, this modality can be an especially powerful paradigm. This, however, comes with the age-old tradeoff of vendor lock-in; if a cloud platform decides to raise the rates on a critical dependency, in the short-term there often isn’t much of a choice other than to absorb those costs.
[35] (P) It should not come as a surprise, then, that there have been some notable examples in the “back-to-on-premise” movement. If workloads are thoroughly understood and predictable, on-premise deployments still can represent not only a cost-effective approach, but one with control down to bare metal if one so chooses.
[36] (P) All of this, whether on-premise or in the public cloud, is part of effective FinOps (financial operational analytics). Whichever deployment path is taken, the responsibility of environment management never ends, and there are no free lunches.

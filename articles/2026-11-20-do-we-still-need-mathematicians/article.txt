[1] (P) When OpenAI claimed on September 8, 2026, that an internal AI system had produced a solution to the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem, it sent shock waves through the mathematical community and far beyond it. Scientific American first ran an article under the headline “The summer AI ate math,” but later changed it to the still rather hyperbolic “If AI can do math, what’s the point of mathematicians?”
[2] (P) The Heidelberg Laureate Forum’s panel discussion “AI in Mathematical Research,” held one week later, could not have come at a better time. Moderated by mathematician Tony Feng of the University of California, Berkeley, the panel brought together Fields Medalists Peter Scholze of Germany’s Max Planck Institute for Mathematics and Jacob Tsimerman of Canada’s University of Toronto, as well as Geordie Williamson of Australia’s University of Sydney, a pioneer in applying AI to mathematical research, and Michael Harris of Columbia University, a prominent number theorist.
[3] (P) Asked how much time they each had spent talking to AI during the previous week, Harris and Scholze both answered: zero. “I’ve never typed anything into a large language model,” Scholze said, adding, “I’m living in a small AI-free bubble, but it’s about to burst, I think.” Williamson estimated his AI-for-math use at three or four hours a day, sometimes leaving AI systems running overnight. Tsimerman said his AI use for math was limited to 30 minutes to an hour a day, which he called “a low week.”
[4] (P) For Tsimerman, who recently joined OpenAI to work on AI safety, AI has become something like a research collaborator. His use has evolved from searching the literature and checking ideas in fields in which he is not an expert, to directing coding agents and sub-agents to explore different approaches. “I would essentially prompt the coding agent with: ‘I think something like this should work. Please spawn several sub-agents: you try this method, you try that method, report back, write a report to me’.”
[5] (P) Tsimerman expects much more to come. “We’re going to get superhuman mathematicians very soon, and mathematics itself will be pushed forward.” Problems that today lie beyond the reach of human researchers may suddenly become accessible as mathematics begins to move “at a superhuman rate,” he said. Rather than fearing that acceleration, he said mathematicians should prepare for this radically new reality.
[6] (P) Scholze questioned the premise that faster mathematics is necessarily better. He compared the seven Millennium Prize Problems to “mountains far out in the mathematical landscape, where we don’t even know the landscape around them.” Their real purpose, he argued, is “to illuminate that landscape, not so much to solve the specific problem.”
[7] (P) The real bottleneck in mathematical research, said Scholze, is not the production of new results, but the ability of mathematicians to develop a shared human understanding of them. “AI wants to accelerate everything, and I don’t see any inherent value in acceleration itself,” he said. “If results accelerate further, there’s just less human understanding.”
[8] (P) Williamson is much more enthusiastic about using AI, but said he sees a related problem. For a long time, solving difficult problems served as a reasonable proxy for mathematical understanding and contribution to the community. AI may now be breaking that link. “Now our community has the challenge to reevaluate how we assess people, who gets jobs, how we educate people et cetera.”
[9] (P) Harris was by far the most critical of the panelists, particularly of commercial AI. He said he refuses to use commercial LLMs, which he sees as appropriating publicly shared intellectual work for private profit. Mathematics has always functioned as a gift economy, he said: researchers freely build on one another’s work as part of a shared intellectual culture.
[10] (P) “The vision of a Humboldtian university,” said Harris, “where the principles are independence from external interference and from subordination to short-term economic considerations, is under attack, specifically from ideologues in Silicon Valley.” In that spirit, he endorses the Leiden Declaration published in June for, among other things, university-based laboratories for automated mathematics.
[11] (P) Some younger mathematicians already are organizing around similar concerns. Students and postdocs recently founded the Association for Human Mathematics. Members pledge either not to work with commercial AI labs and not to use AI to write papers or, more strongly, not to use AI at all. Harris called this “an expression of confidence in human imagination that should be recognized and applauded.”
[12] (P) Tsimerman, however, noted a lack of urgency in the mathematical community. “The world in two years is going to look so incredibly different than it does now,” he said.
[13] (P) Yet predicting superhuman mathematical AI, he stressed, is not the same as declaring human mathematics obsolete. “There’s a big difference between ‘there’s not going to be forefront theorems proven by human mathematicians,’ which is something that I believe is going to be true very soon, and ‘there’s no place for human mathematics,’ which I very much don’t believe.”
[14] (P) Human engagement with mathematics, Tsimerman said, remains valuable as part of human culture. And because AI systems can be unreliable or unaligned with human values, human expertise remains essential: “It’s very hard to have oversight if you don’t have expertise,” he said.
[15] (P) For Williamson, the impact of AI extends far beyond mathematics. “Do we want to construct a world in which human understanding plays no role?” he asked. “I think that I don’t.” Mathematics, he suggested, seems an early test case for what is about to happen across all intellectual work. AI may add a new style of doing mathematics without making the traditional one superfluous. Scholze clearly prefers the traditional style. Calling himself “a dinosaur,” he said he does not even like to sit in front of a computer and type. “I like to think about mathematics on my own, just wander around, talk to others.”
[16] (P) Williamson enjoys using AI to explore new territory. “One of the many things that I adore about mathematics is just the surprise,” he said. “Some problem that you have been working on for some time is solved by a colleague, and the solution is really surprising. And I’ve had a couple of experiences now with AI where it suggests something about a problem that is really surprising, and I love that.”
[17] (P) Bennie Mols is a science and technology writer based in Amsterdam, the Netherlands.

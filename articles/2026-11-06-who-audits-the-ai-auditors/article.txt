[1] (P) I work as an AI assurance specialist at AiEnsured in Bengaluru. My job, in plain terms, is testing other people’s AI. That kind of work used to be optional hygiene. It is steadily becoming an operational requirement: regulators, banks, and large enterprises increasingly want independent verification before a probabilistic system goes anywhere near production.
[2] (P) That is progress. It is also a new risk, and one the industry talks about far less than it should.
[3] (P) The organizations that vouch for other people’s AI now sit on sensitive models, proprietary data and, increasingly, their own AI tooling. Which raises an obvious question that almost nobody in the room wants to answer out loud: who audits the AI auditors?
[4] (H) A Familiar Pattern, Three Familiar Gaps
[5] (P) Computing professionals know this pattern from software quality. Nobody accepts a vendor’s own unit tests as sufficient assurance for a flight-control system. We ask who tested it, against which oracle, with what independence, and what residual risk remains. AI assurance is catching up to that discipline, but unevenly. Three gaps appear again and again in practice.
[6] (P) Independence is assumed, not designed. An internal model-risk team, a consulting slide deck, and an external technical assessor are not interchangeable. Each can be useful. Only some of them can speak freely when the finding is inconvenient. Procurement language still rarely defines what “independent” means for AI testing: contractual freedom to deliver unvarnished findings, separation from the teams that built the model, and clear rules for handling residual risk.
[7] (P) Evidence is thinner than policy. Most enterprises now have AI principles, ethics boards, and acceptable-use documents. Far fewer can show repeatable tests with thresholds, named owners, and continuous monitoring. A fairness metric with no owner and no review cadence is theatre. So is a jailbreak demo that never becomes a regression suite. Assurance that cannot be replayed is very hard to audit.
[8] (P) The auditors use AI, too. Test-case generators, summarizers, and triage assistants bring their own failure modes: client context leaking into external models, overconfident summaries of vulnerability logs, silent drift in the helper model. If an assurance firm cannot inventory and govern its own AI use, buyers should treat that as a material risk, not a footnote.
[9] (H) What the Frameworks Do, and Do Not Do
[10] (P) The regulatory picture is real but partial. The EU AI Act imposes strict independence rules on notified bodies, the third parties that run conformity assessments for certain high-risk systems. Article 31 requires those bodies to be independent of the provider of the system under assessment, of any operator with an economic interest in it, and of the provider’s competitors. Neither the body, its top management, nor the personnel performing the assessment may be involved in the design, development, marketing, or use of the systems they assess, and consultancy work that could compromise independence of judgment is expressly barred. Impartiality procedures and liability insurance are mandatory. That is a serious answer, on paper.
[11] (P) The catch is coverage. For most high-risk uses listed in Annex III, Article 43 routes providers through the internal-control procedure of Annex VI, which means self-assessment with no notified body involved. Third-party assessment is concentrated in narrower cases, mainly certain biometric systems when harmonized standards are not fully applied. The Act hard-codes independence where external assessment is mandatory, and leaves a large share of high-risk systems to the provider’s own processes.
[12] (P) NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework takes the opposite approach: voluntary, process-oriented. Its Measure function recommends involving independent assessors, or internal experts who were not front-line developers, specifically to reduce bias and conflicts of interest. It prescribes no certified auditor class. It treats independent review as a way to strengthen measurement inside an organization’s own risk process, which is useful, and easy to skip.
[13] (P) The standards community is filling part of the gap. ISO/IEC 42001:2023, described by ISO as the world’s first AI management system standard, forces evidence of accountability rather than one-off technical claims: who owns AI risk, how risks are assessed and treated, how changes are controlled, how incidents are learned from. India has adopted it nationally through the Bureau of Indian Standards. Just as relevant to my title question, ISO/IEC 42006:2025 sets requirements for the bodies that audit and certify organizations against ISO/IEC 42001, supplementing the general auditing rules of ISO/IEC 17021-1 with AI-specific expectations on competence, impartiality, and consistency. It defines what the certifier must demonstrate before its stamp means anything. ISO/IEC 23894 adds guidance on AI risk management across the lifecycle. None of this is sufficient on its own, but the shape of an answer is emerging.
[14] (P) Professional and standards bodies have moved in the same direction. IEEE’s 7000-series standards address ethical concerns, transparency, and algorithmic bias in system design. ACM’s Code of Ethics asks computing professionals for comprehensive and thorough evaluation of systems and their impacts, and ACM policy statements call for auditability and independent scrutiny of algorithmic systems. Yet none of these frameworks, on its own or together, establishes one universal regime for overseeing the auditors of everyday AI systems.
[15] (H) The Human Bottleneck
[16] (P) Human judgment is still the strongest tool we have to address ambiguity, accountability, and genuine edge cases. People weigh context that is not in the training data, spot the statistically plausible output that is still unacceptable, and can stand behind a decision when a regulator asks who signed off. An AI system cannot be held responsible in the ordinary sense. Someone has to be.
[17] (P) But judgment does not scale. Ask a person to approve every output or every action, and fatigue and automation bias arrive quickly. People over-trust confident recommendations, click through repetitive prompts, and miss the rare serious error precisely when the system is mostly right. In agentic settings the problem is sharper: an agent can initiate hundreds of actions, and per-action human approval becomes rubber-stamping or simple operational impossibility. Scaling judgment is a design problem, not a staffing slogan. It needs risk-tiered oversight, human-on-the-loop monitoring with selective intervention, clear escalation thresholds, and measurement of the review process itself.
[18] (H) What the Community Should Demand
[19] (P) So what should we, as computing professionals, actually ask for?
[20] (P) Ask assurance providers for the same class of proof we demand of AI vendors. Not a product pitch: evidence. The scope of the assessment, the methods and benchmarks used, the known limitations, the data-handling controls, and how the firm governs AI inside its own delivery. Prefer providers who can show a managed system for AI risk, not only a toolkit. Treat standards such as ISO/IEC 42001, and the competence and impartiality rules of ISO/IEC 42006 for the certifiers themselves, as necessary but not sufficient. They organize accountability. They do not replace technical tests for bias, robustness, security, or drift.
[21] (P) For researchers and tool builders, the opportunity is to make assurance itself measurable: shared benchmarks for LLM application testing, clearer reporting formats for fairness and robustness findings, and evaluation methods that survive model updates. For educators, AI quality belongs next to software engineering and security in the curriculum. The next generation of engineers will ship probabilistic systems. They also will be asked to vouch for them.
[22] (P) I am not arguing that every tester must hold the same certificate, or that management-system audits replace hands-on evaluation. I am arguing for symmetry. As AI assurance moves from optional hygiene to regulatory and contractual necessity, the people who test AI should expect to be tested on how they run their own house.
[23] (P) Trust in AI will not come from louder assurances. It will come from evidence that travels, including evidence about the auditors.

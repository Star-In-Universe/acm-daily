[1] (P) Dear KV,
[2] (P) I have noticed in your recent columns that, like everyone else, you are writing about LLMs and their effect on the practice of programming. As academics who have to teach the next generation of practitioners how to practice, my colleagues and I now find ourselves in the unenviable position of having to go back to the drawing board and work out ways to inculcate the necessary skills while competing with systems that help students complete tasks in considerably less time and with considerably less mental effort. It is well understood that mental effort is required to learn a topic, which is why writing notes by hand continues to result in better learning outcomes than typing them on a laptop or, even worse, absorbing a video lecture.
[3] (P) Since you are an older practitioner who is seeing this transition from the other side, what do you think those of us who are training your replacements should be teaching? Or is it time to let the machines do the work and just give our students our best wishes?
[4] (P) If Wishes Were Horses
[5] (P) Dear Wishes,
[6] (P) It is true that Kode Vicious, like many others in the industry, has been writing about, and more generally dealing with, the LLM craze/fad/bubble—pick a term here. As we have progressed through the past two or so years of this craze, I will call it, the thing that has struck me most is not the increase in our ability to produce more reams of code, but all the hand-wringing on all sides of, “Oh what do we do? What do we do?”
[7] (P) The key skills in software are not typing speed or the visual acuity to spot a missing dereference, but instead they are the skills of software engineering, writ large. What are these skills? They are the ones we were all supposed to be learning, during that one software engineering course everyone was required to take. As KV wrote eons ago, the best thing I ever got out of a software engineering course was reading the Mythical Man-Month by Fred Brooks, and while that book is a deserved classic, it is more about management than about the proper engineering of software artifacts.
[8] (P) While most computing degrees during the past 50 or more years have concentrated on teaching specific technologies (programming in Fortran, or C, or Python, with a side of data structures) or instead went in the other direction into the aerie ether of functional languages and closures, very little time was spent on teaching people how to think about systems.
[9] (P) Now I know that everyone is surprised that KV can be a pedant, but let’s start with the basics. A program is a set of instructions to the computer that get the hardware to do something—hopefully something productive, but at least something. The best programs are made up of libraries, smaller pieces of code that tell the computer to do a more narrowly defined thing. Code, in all its forms, describes algorithms, abstract concepts of how a computer can do a thing. Programs are written in languages that are, supposedly, better at representing the ideas of their human creators than, say, machine code. The keys to making a good software system do not lie in the language specifics or its syntax, although one can make some truly dreadful messes of both, but rather in how the system is put together.
[10] (P) The keys to good software engineering are the interfaces between components, the testability of those components, both for correctness and performance, and how the whole system works together to yield something that is both usable and maintainable over the long term. These are the skills that software engineers need to be trained in—not being able to scan 100 lines of code for a missing asterisk. The computer can do that, and now even faster with the help of LLMs.
[11] (P) When compilers first started replacing assemblers and machine code, they allowed for new levels of abstraction. And it is this topic, abstraction, that we must continue to teach, because it is at the heart of maintainable systems. With the current LLMs, anyone can generate a working program based on a wish—that is, if they wish hard enough (that is, they prompt a lot). But a program is not a system, and the only people KV has seen be successful with these new tools are those who already have the software engineering discipline to know the difference between a program and a system.
[12] (P) How would KV teach such topics? The first thing academia will have to do is to dedicate a lot more time to mentoring interaction, perhaps through teaching assistants who take the time to probe the designs the students show to them. It’s pretty clear that most current programming projects can be solved by the best LLMs without much work from the students. But if you ask the student, “How does it work?”, you will find they often do not know. Finding ways to get them to the point where they can explain how it works is key to training the next group of practitioners.
[13] (P) KV was lucky enough to go to high school in a state that required lab reports for all science courses (biology, chemistry, and physics). Once a week we did an experiment and then had to write it up in a typical “Hypothesis, Experimental Design, Measurements, Evaluation, Conclusions” format. For computer science students, you might just give them the whole program and a set of inputs and ask, “Why?” Now, can an LLM answer this as well? Yes, for simple systems, but it turns out that someone with experience can probe and ask questions the student failed to ask of the LLM since the student does not even know to ask them. A few rounds of this will teach the student what is and is not important in understanding a system—far more than making them implement bubble sort ever would.
[14] (P) Implementing bubble sort does not teach you about when or why to use it, or what the proper interfaces to such an algorithm might be. Questions about sorting algorithms, given the same inputs, and then asking the student to explain why you choose one over the other will actually teach them about algorithmic complexity, performance, and proper APIs.
[15] (P) One of the great teaching advances that is not used often enough is the gigantic corpus of open source software that exists in the world today. It is far more educational to give students a large working system and have them debug it than it is to see if they can each, individually or in pairs, write a sorting algorithm (or whatever other piece of code you would expect a first-year student to crank out over a week or two).
[16] (P) Newly minted practitioners will need a skill that many of us developed naturally, which is to know when the computer is wrong. And this is even more true when using LLMs. People say, “The LLMs lie,” or they “hallucinate,” even though anthropomorphizing computers does not help at all.
[17] (P) The ability to separate the wheat from the chaff is something every software engineer has had to learn how to do, more often on the job than in school. The wheat and the chaff used to come from other programmers. Now it also comes from the computer itself, but the mental skills to deal with it are the same as they ever were. It comes down to knowing how a system looks when it is properly put together. Teach these skills and you, as well as those of us who live long enough to use systems coded by these students, will be grateful. Teach them to spot an errant asterisk, however, and we are all going to be very, very unhappy.
